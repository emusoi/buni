# Direction: decide the look on purpose, and say why

A design that could belong to any product belongs to none. Treat every brief like a studio whose clients have already rejected templated work: make deliberate, specific choices about palette, type and layout that come from this subject, and be able to explain each one. The explanation is part of the design; it is what keeps the next screen, and the next agent, on the same course.

## 1. Read the brief
Before designing anything, answer these. If the brief doesn't say, propose an answer and ask the person to confirm (ask).
- Subject: what is this, concretely? Its industry, materials, objects and vernacular are where distinctive choices come from. A watering app for houseplants and a trading desk can't share a look.
- Audience: who uses it, how often, in what situation (at a desk all day, on a phone in a shop, once a year)?
- The job: what one thing must this surface do?
- Surface mode, which sets what success means:
  - Persuade (landing, pricing, campaign): the visitor decides and acts; the design is the product. Room for boldness.
  - Operate (app, dashboard, editor, settings): the visitor gets a task done. Calm, dense where useful, quick to scan; the brand is quiet.
  - Read (docs, articles, help): the visitor understands. Type and measure lead; nothing competes with the text.
  - Experience (portfolio, gallery, showcase): the visitor inhabits the work; the interface recedes.
- Three dials, each from 1 to 10, said out loud in the plan:
  - Variance: 1 symmetrical and predictable, 10 asymmetric and surprising.
  - Motion: 1 still, 10 cinematic. Operate surfaces stay low.
  - Density: 1 airy like a gallery, 10 packed like a trading screen.
- What already exists: the design doc's decisions, tokens and components. Refining keeps the product's identity and improves its execution; redesigning replaces the visual world but keeps the content, function and conventions people rely on. Say which this is.
- The brief always wins. If it pins a look, era, font or palette, follow it exactly, even where this skill would choose differently.

## Asking well
- Ask about what only the person knows: who it is for, what it must do, the feel they want, what they've rejected before. Never ask what you can decide (spacing, a font pairing) or what the doc already answers.
- One question at a time, with your recommendation first and two or three real alternatives, each saying what it would mean for the design. A question whose options all lead to the same design isn't worth asking.
- Name references in the style vocabulary (styles skill) so the answer is precise: "Swiss and data-dense, like a transit board" beats "clean and modern".

## Samples before commitment
When the direction is open (a new product, a redesign, no look in the brief), show options instead of describing them:
- Pick two or three directions that genuinely differ (different styles and dials, not three shades of one idea), each grounded in the subject and the product's philosophy.
- On a page named "Directions", build the same key moment in each (the hero, the main screen's top, one key card) at small size, side by side, in real content. Label each with its style, its dials and one line on why it fits.
- Screenshot it, then ask which to take, offering a mix as an option. Build the full design only from the chosen one; keep the Directions page so the choice and its alternatives stay on record.
- Skip samples for tweaks, for work inside an existing direction, and when the brief already pins the look.

## 2. Plan in one compact pass
Write a short plan before touching the canvas:
- Colour: 4–6 named hex values with roles ("Loam #2F3A2B, text", "Leaf #4E7A3A, action"). Names from the subject help you remember why.
- Type: the faces and their roles, and the scale. One family is often enough; two must be clearly different. Choose faces for this subject, not the ones you reach for on every project (typography skill).
- Layout: one sentence for the concept, and a quick ASCII sketch or two to compare arrangements. Say how content aligns (left, centred, on a grid) and the density.
- The one bold thing: the memorable element of this design (a type treatment, an image, an interaction, a layout move). Everything else stays quiet and disciplined around it. Spend boldness in one place.
- Style: the movement it works in (styles skill), and any single element borrowed from another, with the reason.
- Principles: two or three beliefs with consequences on what makes this design specifically this product's (philosophies skill).

## 3. Check the plan against the defaults
Before building, ask of every part: is this a choice for this brief, or what I'd produce for any similar brief? Picture the plan for a similar product. If you arrive at the same palette, the same type, the same layout, that part is a default. Revise it, and say what you changed and why. The tells skill lists the common defaults (cream with terracotta, near-black with one acid accent, the SaaS card kit, caps eyebrows and so on). They're legitimate when a brief calls for them; they're failures when they appear by habit.

## 4. Write it down, with the reasons
Record the direction in the design doc (write_section, title "Direction"), so people and agents can read why the design looks the way it does:
- Who it is for and the job, the surface mode and the dials.
- Palette, type and layout, each with one line of reason tied to the subject ("serif display: the care-label feel of plant tags", "low density: people glance at this once a morning").
- The bold thing, and what the design must never feel like.
- What you revised in the check, and why.
Lasting conventions (a radius, the accent's only uses, the spacing base) become decisions (decide), so every later screen follows them. Then set tokens from the plan before building (tokens).

## 5. Build, then critique
- Build with real content in the subject's own words, section by section.
- Screenshot as you go and look: a picture tells you more than the tree. Review against the direction, the principles and the tells, in one batched pass; fix everything you found in one round; check once more; stop (critique skill).
- Before calling it done, take one thing away: the decoration, the extra label, the second accent that doesn't earn its place.
- Hold the quality floor without announcing it: works at every width it must, visible keyboard focus, motion that respects reduced-motion, contrast that passes.

## 6. Explain each design
When you finish, tell the person in a few lines what you made and why: the direction in a sentence, the bold thing, and what to look at. When you change a design later, say what changed and the reason, and update the Direction section if the reasoning moved.
