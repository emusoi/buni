# Styles: vocabularies to choose, not costumes

A style is a set of moves that belong together. Picking one on purpose gives a design coherence; mixing them at random (a glass card on a brutalist page, flat icons beside skeuomorphic buttons) makes it look unsure. Choose the style that serves the subject and the surface (direction skill), then do it properly. Every style below keeps the quality floor: readable contrast, visible focus, real hierarchy, working at every width.

## Minimalism
- What: only what's needed; space, type and one accent do the work.
- Moves: generous whitespace, a strict grid, few colours, a restrained type scale, hierarchy from size and weight, no ornament.
- Fits: tools people use daily, premium products, content that should speak for itself.
- Do it well: reduce to the essence, then make the few elements perfect (type, alignment, spacing). Minimal isn't empty: the remaining things must be rich and specific.
- Fails as: bland grey sameness, low-contrast text, hidden affordances (nothing looks clickable), "minimal" as an excuse for unfinished.

## Swiss (International Typographic Style)
- What: objective, grid-led design from 1950s Switzerland; information through structure.
- Moves: asymmetric layouts on a visible modular grid, sans-serif type at strong scale contrast, flush-left ragged-right text, photography over illustration, colour as signal.
- Fits: information-heavy products, institutions, transport, data, design-literate brands.
- Do it well: let the grid be felt, make type size contrast bold, keep everything aligned to it.
- Fails as: Helvetica-on-white with no grid, a cliché when used as decoration rather than structure.

## Editorial
- What: the logic of magazines and books: reading, pacing and voice.
- Moves: expressive display serifs or grotesques, columns and pull quotes, image-led spreads, varied rhythm between sections, captions.
- Fits: publications, long-form content, brands with stories, culture.
- Do it well: real typographic hierarchy, comfortable measure, images that carry meaning, pacing like turning pages.
- Fails as: broadsheet chrome (hairline rules, zero radius, dense columns) put on a product that isn't editorial (tells skill).

## Brutalism
- What: raw, honest, unpolished, structure exposed.
- Moves: default-looking type, visible borders and boxes, unstyled links, stark contrast, collage, breaking conventions deliberately.
- Fits: art, culture, personal sites, brands that want to provoke.
- Do it well: intentional roughness with clear navigation underneath; every rule broken on purpose.
- Fails as: actually unusable, inaccessible contrast or motion, rough for its own sake.

## Neobrutalism
- What: brutalism made friendly for products: bold, flat, loud and clear.
- Moves: thick black borders (2–4px), hard offset shadows with no blur (4–8px), flat saturated fills, chunky sans type, big obvious buttons that visibly press (the shadow collapses on press).
- Fits: playful consumer apps, creator tools, education, startups that want energy and clarity.
- Do it well: a tight palette (one or two saturated colours plus black and an off-white), consistent border weight and shadow offset everywhere, strong type hierarchy; let the flatness make actions obvious.
- Fails as: hard shadows on a calm product (a tell outside this style), too many colours, every element shouting so nothing leads, low contrast between saturated colours.

## Flat and Material
- What: flat removes ornament; Material adds a system of light, elevation and motion to flatness.
- Moves: solid colour, simple icons, clear elevation levels, meaningful motion, consistent components.
- Fits: apps on Android, enterprise products, anything that needs a robust, familiar system.
- Do it well: use the system's components and elevation rules faithfully, then add a distinct colour and type.
- Fails as: the generic default app, ambiguous flat buttons with no affordance.

## Skeuomorphism
- What: imitating real materials and objects (leather, paper, knobs, gauges).
- Moves: textures, realistic lighting, physical controls that behave like their objects.
- Fits: instruments, audio and music tools, games, tactile niche products, kids.
- Do it well: realism that teaches how something works (a dial turns), with crisp detail.
- Fails as: heavy textures that slow everything and age badly; realism without function.

## Glassmorphism
- What: translucent, blurred layers over colourful backgrounds.
- Moves: backdrop blur, semi-opaque fills, thin light borders, depth from layering.
- Fits: overlays and chrome that must keep context visible (OS-level panels, media controls, sticky bars over imagery).
- Do it well: use it for layers that float over content, with enough fill to keep text contrast.
- Fails as: glass cards as decoration on a plain page (a tell), unreadable text over busy backgrounds.

## Neumorphism and claymorphism
- What: soft extruded shapes from paired light and dark shadows (neumorphism); puffy rounded 3D shapes with inner highlights (clay).
- Fits: small, tactile, consumer moments (a single control, an illustration style), smart-home or playful brands.
- Do it well: use it for a few hero elements with strong contrast added back for text and focus.
- Fails as: whole interfaces with no contrast where buttons and inputs can't be told apart; a serious accessibility problem.

## Bento
- What: content in a grid of differently sized tiles, like a bento box.
- Moves: one strong grid, tiles of varying size by importance, each tile one idea, consistent gap and radius.
- Fits: product overviews, feature summaries, personal dashboards, portfolios.
- Do it well: size tiles by importance and give each real content (a live demo, an image, a number with meaning).
- Fails as: identical feature cards with icons pretending to be bento (the SaaS card kit).

## Retro, Y2K and vaporwave
- What: references to a period's technology and culture (pixel UIs, chrome, early web, 80s neon).
- Fits: entertainment, music, fashion, nostalgia-led brands, campaigns.
- Do it well: research the period's real artefacts; keep modern usability under the nostalgia.
- Fails as: pastiche, unreadable effects, references that don't relate to the subject.

## Maximalism
- What: more is more: layered colour, pattern, type and imagery with intent.
- Fits: fashion, music, culture, events, brands defined by abundance.
- Do it well: a strong underlying grid and hierarchy so the richness has order; one clear path to action.
- Fails as: noise; nothing leads; slow pages.

## Organic and natural
- What: forms, colours and textures from nature: soft shapes, earthy palettes, hand-made marks.
- Fits: wellness, food, sustainability, craft, outdoors.
- Do it well: take colours and shapes from the actual subject (this soil, this wood), keep layouts calm and structured.
- Fails as: generic beige and blobs; organic shapes that fight the grid.

## Bauhaus and Memphis
- What: Bauhaus: primary colours, geometric shapes, function shaping form. Memphis: playful clashing patterns, squiggles and bright geometry from the 80s.
- Fits: culture, education, creative tools, playful brands (Memphis); design-led institutions (Bauhaus).
- Do it well: geometry with a reason (grouping, direction), a strict palette.
- Fails as: shapes scattered as decoration with no structure.

## Dark luxury
- What: deep backgrounds, restrained metallic or jewel accents, refined type, slow pace.
- Fits: premium goods, hospitality, automotive, night-time products.
- Do it well: real depth in the darks (not flat near-black), very few accents, generous space, exquisite type.
- Fails as: near-black with one acid accent (a tell), low contrast for the sake of mood.

## Data-dense (professional tools)
- What: the interface of people who work in it all day: trading, monitoring, analytics, IDEs.
- Moves: compact rows, tabular numbers, small precise type, colour reserved for state, keyboard everything, minimal chrome.
- Fits: operate surfaces with expert users.
- Do it well: density with strict alignment and hierarchy; every pixel of colour means something.
- Fails as: dashboards of decorative charts, metrics nobody acts on.

## Mixing
- One style leads; at most one other contributes a single element with a reason (a minimal product with one neobrutalist call to action for a campaign). Record the choice in the Direction section.
- Whatever the style, the content and the subject come first: the style is how they're presented, not what replaces them.
