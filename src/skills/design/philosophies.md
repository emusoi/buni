# Philosophies: what a product believes about design

Styles are how a product looks; a philosophy is what it believes. Great products have a small number of clear beliefs that settle hundreds of small decisions. Know the classic ones, then write the product's own (in the design doc's principles), specific enough to decide between two designs.

## Dieter Rams: less, but better
Good design is innovative, makes a product useful, is aesthetic, makes a product understandable, is unobtrusive, is honest, is long-lasting, is thorough down to the last detail, is environmentally friendly, and is as little design as possible.
- Use it to: cut what doesn't serve use; refuse fashionable tricks that will date; finish the details nobody asked for.

## Apple: clarity, deference, depth (the iOS 7 era guidelines)
- Clarity: text legible, icons precise, function obvious.
- Deference: the interface helps people understand and use content, and stays out of its way.
- Depth: layers and motion convey hierarchy and where things came from.
- Use it to: let content lead, keep chrome quiet, use motion and layers to explain structure.

## Material: a metaphor of surfaces and light (Material Design 1)
- Interfaces as surfaces with real behaviour: elevation, shadow and motion that respond to people.
- Use it to: build a consistent system of elevation and motion, and make every response physical and meaningful.

## Calm technology
- Technology should need the smallest possible amount of attention; inform without demanding focus; use the periphery; work even when it fails.
- Use it to: design notifications, reminders and status for products people live with (a plant reminder, a thermostat), not stare at.

## Tufte: show the data
- Maximise the share of ink that shows data; remove chartjunk; show comparisons, causality and context; small multiples over one cluttered chart; integrate words, numbers and images.
- Use it to: design dashboards and reports that answer questions rather than decorate.

## Don Norman: design for how people think
- Affordances (what something lets you do) and signifiers (how you know); mappings that match the world (a control near what it controls); feedback for every action; constraints that prevent errors; a clear conceptual model.
- Use it to: make actions discoverable and mistakes hard, and to explain why a design confuses.

## Ma and kanso: space and simplicity
- Ma: the meaningful interval: emptiness as part of the composition. Kanso: simplicity by eliminating clutter.
- Use it to: give elements room to be understood; treat space as content, not leftovers.

## Joy and personality
- The counterweight to austerity: products people love have moments of warmth, wit and craft (a delightful empty state, a satisfying confirmation).
- Use it to: add personality where it rewards people without slowing them; one memorable detail per experience.

## Jobs to be done
- People hire a product to make progress in a situation ("help me keep my plants alive without thinking about it"). Design for the job and the situation, not features.
- Use it to: decide what's on the first screen and what's absent.

## Honesty and respect
- No dark patterns (hidden costs, confirm-shaming, roach motels, fake urgency, pre-ticked boxes). Make leaving as easy as joining, prices and consequences clear, and defaults in the person's favour.
- Use it to: judge every growth idea; a design that tricks people is a broken design.

## Writing the product's own philosophy
- Three to five principles in the design doc, each a belief with a consequence: "Glanceable over complete: the Today screen shows what to do, history waits a tap away." "The plant decides, not the calendar: we remind to check the soil, never to water."
- A good principle helps choose between two good designs; "simple and beautiful" doesn't, "fewer screens over fewer taps" does.
- Name the classic it leans on when it helps the team ("calm technology: reminders arrive once a day, never as a stream").
- Review designs against these principles in critique; when a design must break one, record why (decide).
