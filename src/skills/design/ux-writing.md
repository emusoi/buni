# UX writing

Words appear in a design for one reason: to make it easier to understand and use. They are design content, not decoration. Bring the same intention and minimalism to copy that you bring to spacing and colour. Copy can make a design feel as templated as its layout.

## Before writing
- Ask what this screen needs to say, and how it can best be said to help someone move through it. Then write only that.
- Use the brief's real content and subject throughout. Lorem ipsum and generic filler hide length, tone and hierarchy problems.

## The person's words
- Name things by what people understand, not by how the system is built: someone manages notifications, not webhook config.
- Describe what something is or does in plain terms rather than selling it. Specific and legible beats clever.
- Avoid the generated-copy vocabulary: unlock, seamless, supercharge, elevate, effortless, "the future of" (tells skill).

## Actions
- Active voice. A button says exactly what happens: "Save changes", not "Submit"; "Delete 3 files", not "OK".
- An action keeps its name through the whole flow: the "Publish" button leads to a "Published" toast and a "Published" badge. The interface's vocabulary is the signposting people learn their way around by.
- Link text makes sense out of context, never "click here".

## Headlines
- Say the job or the benefit specifically ("Know which plants need a check today") rather than a slogan ("Welcome to smarter plant care").
- One headline per screen or section; the subhead explains what the headline promises.

## Failure and emptiness give direction, not mood
- Errors say what happened and how to fix it, in the interface's voice: "That card was declined. Try another card or call your bank." They don't apologise and are never vague; no raw codes on their own; never blame the person.
- An empty screen is an invitation to act: what will appear here and the first action to get there ("No plants yet. Add your first plant.").

## Tone and style
- Conversational: plain verbs, no filler, tone matched to the brand and the audience. Calm for tools people use all day; warmer for consumer moments.
- Sentence case for headings, buttons and labels (unless the brand's style says otherwise; then be consistent).
- Talk to the person ("your plants"), from the product's voice; not "I" or "we" in interface text.
- Each written element does exactly one job; if a label and a helper say the same thing, cut one.
- Numerals for counts ("8 plants"), consistent dates, times and currency for the reader's locale, a space between a number and its unit ("10 MB"), curly quotes and the ellipsis character.
